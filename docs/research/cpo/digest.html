CPO digest

A plain-language guide to the problem, the technology and the companies

Author

Nur Alpys

Published

August 19, 2026

The short version

AI data centres are pushing more data through every switch. The old answer—put a replaceable optical module on the front of the switch—still works, but the electrical connection between the switch chip and that module becomes harder to drive at higher speeds. Co-packaged optics (CPO) moves the optical engine next to the switch chip to shorten that electrical path.

That may reduce power and improve density. It also makes the switch harder to manufacture, qualify and repair. The central question is therefore not whether CPO can work in a demonstration. It is whether a qualified CPO system can beat the best pluggable, LPO or NPO alternative after yield, service and replacement costs.

1. Why the industry needs a new interconnect

The problem is a chain of linked constraints. CPO addresses some of them, but transfers difficulty into the package and manufacturing process.

Problem A — the electrical path is becoming expensive

At high lane rates, data travels from the switch ASIC through the package, circuit board, connectors and front-panel module before becoming light. That path consumes signal margin and power. CPO places the optical engine close to the ASIC, reducing the length of the most demanding electrical connection (Optical Internetworking Forum 2023, 2024).

What would solve it: shorten the electrical path, or make the existing path efficient enough with retimers, LPO, better equalisation or active electrical cables.

Why CPO is not automatically the winner: a shorter path is valuable only if its manufacturing and service burden is lower than the electrical burden it removes.

The boundary becomes more important as the lane rate rises. At 200G/lane, Broadcom’s BCM78919 and NVIDIA’s Spectrum-X Ethernet Photonics show that vendors are defining a product around a short electrical path. At 400G/lane, the same logic becomes more sensitive to package loss, bump-to-bump loss and reach assumptions. A laboratory result at one loss boundary cannot settle the architecture decision for every switch (Broadcom 2025b; NVIDIA 2026a; Zhou et al. 2026).

For an investor, the useful mental model is a shrinking signal budget. Each connector, via, trace and package transition consumes part of that budget; equalisation and retiming recover margin, but at the cost of power, latency, heat and board complexity. CPO removes some transitions by moving the electro-optic conversion closer to the ASIC. It does not remove the need for a complete optical link budget, thermal design or field-replacement plan. The architecture wins only when the saved electrical margin is worth more than the integration burden.
Problem B — power and density are becoming system constraints

Every switch port has a power budget. At very high port counts, the optics, DSPs, electrical losses and cooling can consume space and energy that operators would rather use for compute. NVIDIA and Broadcom both present CPO as a route to higher-bandwidth AI networking, but their public material does not provide a matched, independently measured total-system comparison (NVIDIA 2025; Broadcom 2025a).

What would solve it: demonstrate lower delivered power at the same lane rate, reach, throughput and cooling boundary—not just a lower component number.

The report’s 102.4T thought experiment illustrates the scale without pretending to be a chassis measurement: 64 × 1.6T ports at an assumed 7.2 W CPO interface use 460.8 W, versus 1,536 W at 24 W for a fully retimed interface. An 8 W LPO case is only about 10% above the assumed CPO interface. The conclusion is conditional: CPO has a large advantage against a heavily retimed boundary, but a much smaller advantage against an efficient linear or near-packaged alternative (NVIDIA 2025; Marvell 2025).

Power must be measured at a common boundary. A component pJ/bit figure can omit the external laser, host SerDes, voltage conversion, cooling and endpoint optics. Conversely, a switch-level number can include loads that every architecture shares. The meaningful test is delivered facility power for the same throughput, reach, FEC, cooling method and replacement policy. Until that comparison is disclosed, the calculation is a sensitivity analysis rather than a claim that every CPO chassis saves 70%.
Problem C — dense optical assembly is difficult to manufacture

An optical engine combines a PIC, drivers and receivers, laser light, fibre connections, thermal structures, package materials and test. A defect found late can scrap far more value than a defect found at wafer level. Public manufacturing sources map wafer probing, optical assembly, package test and burn-in, but do not disclose a full production yield waterfall (ASE 2025; Teradyne 2026; Advantest 2026; ficonTEC 2025; Aehr Test Systems 2026).

What would solve it: test earlier, use known-good components, automate alignment and make rework possible without destroying the rest of the engine.

The practical flow is wafer probe → die or PIC screening → optical alignment and fibre attach → engine test → package integration → module or switch test → burn-in and qualification. A defect found before attach may consume a die; the same defect found after attach can consume an engine, package, labour and test time. Public equipment descriptions establish that these are distinct process insertions, but they do not publish the stage-by-stage yield or rework waterfall needed to calculate qualified cost (ASE 2025; Teradyne 2026; Advantest 2026; ficonTEC 2025; Aehr Test Systems 2026).

The manufacturing question is therefore not simply “can the machine align fibres?” It is whether alignment stays stable across temperature, vibration and reflow; whether a marginal engine can be reworked; whether test coverage catches latent optical and electrical defects; and whether the line can repeat the result at volume. Known-good-die screening and wafer-level probing can move failures earlier, but they add equipment, data and cycle time. The winning process minimises qualified cost, not just assembly time.
Problem D — replacing a failed part may be harder

A front-panel optical module can normally be replaced without replacing the switch. A deeply integrated engine may not be independently replaceable. External-laser and detachable-interface standards can separate part of the failure domain, but they do not automatically make the PIC or package serviceable (Optical Internetworking Forum 2025; Psaila et al. 2023).

What would solve it: define which part can be replaced in the field, how long the repair takes, what spares are needed and who carries the warranty cost.

This is a system-design choice, not a minor connector detail. A detachable external-laser or fibre interface may isolate one failure domain, while a failed PIC, interposer or bonded optical engine may still require replacement of a larger assembly. The relevant operating metrics are mean time to repair, spare-module policy, escape rate and warranty allocation—not merely whether a connector can be unplugged (Optical Internetworking Forum 2025; Psaila et al. 2023).

The service boundary also changes inventory economics. A replaceable laser may require a small, qualified spare; a non-replaceable engine can require an entire switch tray or chassis intervention. Operators will trade lower power against truck rolls, spares, downtime and warranty exposure. Public CPO disclosures rarely report these costs, so serviceability remains a first-order adoption variable rather than a footnote.
Problem E — technical success does not identify the profit winner

CPO can move content from a pluggable module into the optical engine, laser, fibre interface, package and test flow. That changes where work happens, but not automatically who earns the profit. Supplier share, realised price, yield, rework and warranty remain mostly undisclosed.

What would solve it: connect a named product to a named supplier, qualified volume, content share and margin after manufacturing costs.

The economic chain is: architecture need → qualified engine → attributable supplier content → yield and rework → realised gross profit. A supplier can own an essential technical step and still earn weak returns if the step is multi-sourced, capital-intensive, exposed to price-down or burdened by warranty. That is why the report refuses to treat a partnership, demonstration or capacity announcement as proof of profit capture.

The value pool can also migrate rather than grow. CPO may reduce the bill for a conventional pluggable DSP while increasing content in PICs, lasers, fibre interfaces, advanced packages, automated alignment and test. If several suppliers qualify interchangeable parts, the content may be strategically important but economically competitive. A durable profit pool requires scarcity, qualification friction, switching cost or system-level control—not simply a larger component count.

2. What stage is the industry at?

The industry is beyond basic laboratory curiosity, but short of public commercial proof.

Stage 1 — component and laboratory evidence

Academic and vendor demonstrations show that high-speed PICs, optical engines, external lasers, packaging and test methods can work at bounded conditions. These results establish technical routes, not customer adoption or production economics (Amiralizadeh et al. 2025; Wu et al. 2026; Tran et al. 2026).

The distinction matters in plain language: a measured optical package can prove that a coupling or modulation method works; it cannot prove that thousands of engines pass final test at an acceptable cost. The strongest academic results in the evidence set therefore support the engineering feasibility of the building blocks while leaving commercial scale, service and margin as open questions.

This stage is still valuable. It narrows which mechanisms deserve manufacturing attention: external-light strategies, PIC/EIC co-integration, fibre coupling, thermal management and test-before-integration. It does not rank vendors, because a paper generally reports a device or test vehicle rather than a complete customer product with warranty and field data.
Stage 2 — defined products and platforms

Broadcom has defined a 102.4T Tomahawk 6/Davisson CPO route, and NVIDIA describes Spectrum-X Ethernet Photonics as a 200G-SerDes CPO system (Broadcom 2025b; NVIDIA 2026a). This is stronger than a lab demo because a product boundary exists.

Product definition also narrows the diligence question. The relevant question is no longer “can CPO be built?” but “which configuration is actually being sold, to whom, in what quantity, and with what service boundary?” A broad switch-family announcement does not answer those questions for the CPO configuration.

This is why the digest names the product routes but avoids converting them into market-share forecasts. A specified 102.4T switch or 200G-SerDes photonics system establishes that the vendor has committed engineering and product resources. It does not reveal whether customers receive a CPO configuration, a pluggable configuration, or both under the same platform label.
Stage 3 — production signals, but not complete commercial proof

NVIDIA uses “in production” language for Spectrum-X Ethernet Photonics, and Broadcom reports production-volume shipping for the broader Tomahawk 6 family. Public evidence still does not join an exact CPO SKU to a named customer, accepted unit range, repeat shipment, qualified yield or supplier economics. The report therefore treats this as early production evidence with an open commercial gate.

That is a positive timing signal, not a negative technology verdict. It means the public record has crossed from component proof into vendor-declared product activity, while the evidence needed to estimate market share or supplier earnings remains unavailable. The key risk is that “production” resolves to evaluation systems, a wider platform family, or configurations that still use pluggable optics.

The distinction is especially important for company analysis. NVIDIA can benefit from platform differentiation even if optical-engine economics sit with suppliers. Broadcom can ship a switch family in volume without disclosing the share of CPO configurations. Coherent, Lumentum, TSMC and test suppliers can have strong technical roles without a public record of allocation or realised margin. The public evidence therefore supports exposure rankings, not a proven earnings winner.
What would count as commercial proof?

The decisive record would combine a clear CPO SKU, named customer, accepted unit or port range, repeat shipment, supplier/content boundary and at least one qualified-yield, service or margin metric. Until that appears, “production” is a positive timing signal—not proof of market leadership.

The evidence gate is cumulative. A customer name without a CPO SKU is insufficient; a SKU without accepted units is insufficient; accepted units without repetition may still be a pilot; repetition without qualified yield or service data does not establish economic scale. This is the standard used throughout the full report.

There are three useful outcomes from the gate. Pass: customer-confirmed, repeat paid production with an attributable supplier and qualified economics. Partial pass: a product is shipping, but units, service or supplier economics remain opaque. Fail for investment purposes: the claimed production resolves to a demo, evaluation quantity, non-CPO configuration or an architecture that is abandoned after qualification. A partial pass can support a timing view without supporting a profit-pool call.

3. Architecture choices

These options solve different parts of the problem. The best choice depends on lane rate, reach, power, serviceability and the operator’s tolerance for integration risk.

Retimed pluggables — the mature baseline

What they do: keep the optical module replaceable and put more signal-processing electronics inside it.

Why they can win: excellent serviceability, mature supply chain and clear field replacement.

Where they struggle: module power, electrical reach, front-panel density and cooling at very high lane rates.

Best fit: links where modular replacement matters more than the last increment of density or power efficiency.

Retimed optics are the control case because they preserve the cleanest operational model. Their weakness is not that they stop working; it is that DSP, host electrical reach and front-panel power can become expensive at the same bandwidth. If those costs remain manageable, the modular baseline can defeat a technically superior but harder-to-service CPO design.

The comparison should include the full replacement burden. Retimed modules may be individually expensive and power-hungry, but they can be swapped without disturbing the switch ASIC, package or neighbouring links. That operational advantage has an economic value which is often absent from optical-engine power demonstrations. CPO must overcome this value through density, power, reach or a lower qualified-cost curve.
LPO — the power countercase

What it does: removes or reduces module DSP and relies on the host channel and system equalisation to carry the link.

Why it can win: lower module power while keeping a replaceable module.

Where it struggles: tighter host-channel margin, interoperability and reach requirements.

Best fit: systems where the electrical channel is good enough that deep package integration is unnecessary.

LPO therefore tests whether the host channel—not the optical engine—is the real bottleneck. It can retain a replaceable module while reducing module electronics, but it transfers more responsibility to board design, equalisation, interoperability and system validation. A fair comparison must hold lane rate, reach, FEC and cooling boundary constant.

LPO is strongest where the switch package and board can maintain enough margin without a retimer. It is weakest where channel variation, connector loss or system reach forces conservative equalisation and lowers manufacturing yield. The relevant evidence is not a low module-power headline alone; it is interoperability across hosts, stable error performance and field qualification at the target lane rate.
NPO — the intermediate route

What it does: places optics nearer to the ASIC than a front-panel module, without necessarily integrating every optical function into the ASIC package.

Why it can win: some electrical and power benefit with fewer service penalties than deep CPO.

Where it struggles: it can introduce its own package, connector, thermal and cable-management complexity.

Best fit: deployments that need a shorter electrical path but still value an intermediate replacement boundary (China Mobile and IEEE 802.3 400G per Lane Study Group 2026; Optical Internetworking Forum 2026).

NPO is not simply “CPO later.” It can be a durable compromise if it captures enough electrical benefit while keeping an engine, tray or connector replaceable. Its risk is that it creates a new package and cable-management boundary without obtaining the full electrical advantage of deep integration (China Mobile and IEEE 802.3 400G per Lane Study Group 2026; Optical Internetworking Forum 2026).

NPO can also change who controls the platform. A detachable engine or optical tray may give an engine supplier more content while leaving the switch owner responsible for the package and service interface. That can make NPO attractive when operators value supplier choice, but the intermediate boundary must be standardised enough that customers do not inherit a bespoke maintenance model.
Switch-side CPO — the deepest integration route

What it does: places the optical engine next to the switch ASIC, shortening the highest-speed electrical path.

Why it can win: maximum electrical reach relief, high port density and potentially lower interface power.

Where it struggles: fibre attach, thermal integration, final test, rework, serviceability and supplier attribution.

Best fit: high-bandwidth Ethernet fabrics where the electrical and power constraints are binding enough to justify the integration burden.

The strongest public timing signal is at 200G/lane switch-side Ethernet, where product routes are specified by both NVIDIA and Broadcom. At 400G/lane, the engineering pressure is stronger but the commercial evidence is thinner; loss, thermal and package assumptions still determine whether CPO or NPO/LPO is the better system boundary (Broadcom 2025a, 2026b; Zhou et al. 2026).

Deep integration concentrates both upside and failure risk. It can reduce the longest electrical path and improve front-panel density, but it also couples optical alignment, thermal expansion, package stress and switch availability. The adoption threshold is crossed only when operators accept that coupling because the alternative cannot meet the same bandwidth and power envelope at an acceptable service cost.
Accelerator optical I/O — adjacent, not a direct CPO competitor

Optical I/O connects accelerator chips, packages or scale-up fabrics. It uses related PIC, laser and packaging technologies, but serves a different topology, customer and failure domain. It should not be combined with switch-side CPO in one adoption forecast.

Why it matters here: Marvell and some component suppliers may gain more from scale-up optical I/O than from Ethernet switch CPO. It is an adjacent value-pool opportunity, not a replacement for the switch-CPO comparison (Marvell 2025; Marvell Technology 2026).

The distinction protects the analysis from double counting. Switch-side CPO is bought as an Ethernet networking platform; optical I/O is tied to accelerator packages, scale-up fabrics and a different software and system architecture. The PIC, laser and packaging technologies overlap, but the customer denominator, product cycle and failure domain do not.

Optical I/O can still matter to the same suppliers. A PIC or laser vendor may sell into both tracks, while a platform company may have very different strategic exposure in each. The correct method is to maintain separate adoption curves and then ask whether shared supplier content is material enough to change the company-level conclusion.

4. Which companies matter?

The companies do not all offer the same exposure. “CPO success” could benefit a platform owner, an optical-engine supplier, a laser supplier or a packaging manufacturer—and those are different investment cases.

NVIDIA — platform control

NVIDIA has the strongest integrated route: switch silicon, networking systems, software, customer relationships and a stated CPO production path. The upside is architecture control and platform differentiation. The unknown is how much incremental CPO profit remains with NVIDIA rather than its suppliers (NVIDIA 2026a, 2026b).

View: positive strategic exposure, medium confidence. What would strengthen it: customer-confirmed CPO SKU, repeat deployment and system economics.

NVIDIA’s control point is integration: it can coordinate switch silicon, networking software, rack design and the customer deployment even if suppliers manufacture the optical engine. That makes it the clearest platform route, but not automatically the largest CPO content or margin beneficiary. The view is weakened if announced photonics systems are mainly pluggable configurations or if service and yield costs limit deployment (NVIDIA 2026a, 2026b).

For a reader deciding what to monitor, the highest-value NVIDIA evidence is a product-linked customer acceptance record that distinguishes CPO from adjacent Spectrum-X configurations. The next layer is repeat deployment and evidence that CPO improves rack density, system ASP or customer lock-in. Consolidated NVIDIA earnings cannot be treated as CPO earnings without that bridge.
Broadcom — direct merchant-switch exposure

Broadcom has the clearest merchant switch-silicon and CPO product-definition route. If switch-side CPO becomes a standard high-end Ethernet option, Broadcom could influence the ASIC, optical interface and ecosystem qualification. The unknown is whether the optical content is economically meaningful to Broadcom (Broadcom 2025a, 2026b).

View: positive enabling exposure, medium confidence. What would strengthen it: exact CPO customer deployment, repeat shipments and differentiated content or margin evidence.

Broadcom’s control point is merchant silicon and the product interface: its CPO route can influence the engine specification, ecosystem qualification and customer reference design. The public gap is unusually important because Tomahawk 6 production-volume language covers a broader family than the exact Davisson/BCM78919 CPO configuration. A stronger case requires external evidence that the CPO route itself is shipping repeatedly (Broadcom 2025b, 2026a).

Broadcom’s upside is leverage rather than necessarily optical content. If customers standardise on the merchant CPO interface, Broadcom may protect switch-silicon share and shape qualification even when an external supplier builds the engine. The downside is that the optical route becomes pass-through content, while customer service and yield risk remain with the platform ecosystem.
Coherent — broad optical-engine and PIC option value

Coherent spans silicon photonics, InP lasers, VCSELs, external lasers and packaging. That gives it more ways to participate if the industry adopts several engine designs. The risk is that demonstrations and technology breadth do not prove qualified share or margin (Coherent 2026a, 2026b).

View: constructive component exposure, medium confidence. What would strengthen it: a named design win, defined content boundary and repeat qualified revenue.

Coherent’s advantage is optionality across the engine stack: PICs, InP and VCSEL sources, external lasers and packaging. Optionality has value when several architectures compete, but it can also mean that no single route earns durable share. The investment case improves only when breadth converts into a product-linked, qualified and economically visible role (Coherent 2026a, 2026b).

The key diligence question is which layer Coherent controls in the final qualified engine. A capability list can span many technologies without proving that one customer product uses them, that Coherent supplies the scarce step, or that the step carries attractive gross margin. Design-win disclosure and repeat qualified revenue matter more than the number of demonstrations.
Lumentum — external-laser conversion

Lumentum is most relevant if external lasers become a scarce, replaceable and valuable CPO boundary. Its high-power laser work and NVIDIA partnership strengthen the strategic signal, but the public record does not yet show finished-module volume or CPO-specific margin (Lumentum 2026b, 2026c, 2026a).

View: constructive/watch, medium confidence. What would strengthen it: product-linked laser content, volume, realised price and margin.

Lumentum is a focused bet on the external-light boundary. A detachable laser can improve serviceability and isolate a high-value failure domain, but it may also be multi-sourced and exposed to price pressure. The decisive evidence is not another laser demonstration; it is product-linked volume, content per engine, qualification and margin (Lumentum 2026a, 2026c).

The upside case is a laser that is difficult to qualify, replaceable in the field and scarce enough to command content. The downside case is a qualified component with several sources and little pricing power. Lumentum’s reliability work improves confidence in the device, but it does not establish finished-engine reliability or CPO-specific earnings.
TSMC — process and packaging control

TSMC matters as a possible manufacturing control point for silicon photonics and advanced packaging. It is not a directly comparable merchant optical-engine supplier, and CPO economics may remain invisible inside its broader foundry business (TSMC 2024, 2026a, 2026b).

View: infrastructure watch, medium confidence. What would strengthen it: programme-level production throughput, constrained capacity or pricing-power evidence.

TSMC’s control point is manufacturability: silicon photonics, heterogeneous integration and advanced packaging can determine which designs reach volume. That position may be strategically important while remaining financially hard to isolate in a large foundry. A production-start statement is therefore a process-readiness signal, not evidence of CPO revenue or pricing power (TSMC 2024, 2026a, 2026b).

TSMC can win even if no merchant CPO component becomes a large standalone business, because process control may determine which platforms are manufacturable. Conversely, a broad foundry capability can be strategically useful without creating scarcity or margin. The evidence to watch is capacity allocation, customer-specific process qualification and signs that the package flow is difficult to substitute.
Marvell — separate optical-I/O optionality

Marvell’s most relevant route is accelerator optical I/O and Photonic Fabric, not switch-side CPO. It could benefit if scale-up optical connectivity becomes a large market, but switch-CPO production evidence should not be used as evidence for Marvell’s optical-I/O revenue (Marvell 2025; Marvell Technology 2026).

View: strategic watch, low-to-medium confidence. What would strengthen it: named optical-I/O product, customer adoption and realised revenue or margin evidence.

Marvell should be evaluated on the optical-I/O track rather than awarded switch-CPO credit. Celestial AI and Photonic Fabric may become strategically important if scale-up bandwidth and package reach become binding, but that market has a different adoption curve. The correct evidence is a named optical-I/O product and customer revenue, not a switch-CPO production headline (Marvell 2025; Marvell Technology 2026).

Marvell is therefore an optionality case, not a direct switch-CPO leader. Its upside depends on optical I/O becoming a repeatable platform product with a customer denominator and content model that can be separated from the broader acquisition and connectivity portfolio. Until then, the appropriate stance remains strategic watch.

Bottom line

If CPO succeeds, the likely beneficiaries divide into four groups:

  1. Platform control: NVIDIA and Broadcom.
  2. Optical-engine/PIC content: Coherent and specialist photonics suppliers.
  3. External-light content: Lumentum and Coherent.
  4. Manufacturing control: TSMC, ASE and advanced package/test partners.

The strongest direct switch-CPO exposure is Broadcom; the broadest engine/PIC optionality is Coherent; the clearest platform exposure is NVIDIA; and Lumentum is the focused external-laser case. Those are conditional views, not target prices. The full report explains what evidence would confirm or invalidate each one.
